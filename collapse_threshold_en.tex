\begin{abstract}
We propose a modification of quantum mechanics through the introduction of the \CollapseRule{}: the Born weight of a branch is quantized in steps no smaller than $\varepsilon=1/\Ost$, so a branch with weight below~$\varepsilon$ does not exist, and outcome probabilities are counted among the existing branches. A branch is one of the simultaneously existing terms of a state; the threshold does not act on the product of the weights of separate systems. Objectivation occurs when the residual coherence falls below the same threshold~$\varepsilon$. The threshold follows from $\Lambda$CDM cosmology: a finite number of binary distinctions can be executed within a causal patch, $\Ost=3.5\times 10^{120}$. The wall of equiprobable branching and the RCS limit lie no higher than about $400$ qubits; where exactly is for experiment to determine. Four experiments on a single processor are proposed. In the main one, the local marginal test, the probability of finding one qubit in the state one falls from~$1/2$ to zero; $410$ qubits are certainly sufficient for it. The same experiment on a separated baseline gives superluminal information transfer. Truncation and selection are non-unitary and permit non-conservation of energy.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} objective collapse; objectivation; vanishing of branches; residual coherence; coherent probability; einselection; computational limit; $\Lambda$CDM cosmology; random circuit sampling; Shor's algorithm; superluminal telephone; CMB frame

\section{Introduction}

Standard quantum mechanics permits unitary decoherence: the reduced entropy of a subsystem grows, the global entropy is zero, and the phase is recorded in correlations with the environment and is recoverable (Loschmidt echo~\cite{swingle2018}). A classical bit is thereby not born \emph{objectively}: the outcome is definite only as an interpretation, not as a fact.

The Copenhagen interpretation separates a microscopic system that is in a superposition from a classical apparatus, but does not say where the Heisenberg cut lies and does not fix a numerical boundary. Decoherence and einselection select a pointer basis and explain the stability of macroscopic pointers~\cite{zurek2003}, yet by themselves they do not specify at what branching width a superposition remains physically distinguishable. The aim of this work is to locate that cut quantitatively and to work out the consequences.

The standard theory does not set a minimum nonzero value for density-matrix elements and probability weights. Decoherence can reduce coherences without bound, and sequential branching and the spatial tails of the wave function create arbitrarily small nonzero weights. If such quantities are treated as independently distinguishable physical data and stored with a fixed absolute resolution, the required bit depth grows linearly with time for exponential decay and quadratically with distance for a Gaussian tail. Without a lower threshold it is unbounded and, given a sufficiently long evolution, exceeds any finite information limit of the region, in particular the Bekenstein holographic bound~\cite{bousso2002}. Mathematically, arbitrarily small coefficients are admissible, but storing and distinguishing them physically at finite information capacity is impossible. Hence the idea of an information threshold below which values are physically indistinguishable.

The information that can be obtained and verified experimentally is finite in two independent senses. The first limit is set by quantum uncertainty. Incompatible physical quantities cannot be measured simultaneously with arbitrarily high precision; with gravity taken into account, even a single quantity cannot be measured with arbitrarily high precision: the precision of a measurement of a length or of a time interval is bounded by the K\'arolyh\'azy limit~\cite{karolyhazy1966}. The second sets the maximum number of experiments---questions that nature answers. It is tied to the finiteness of the causal patch---the maximal causally closed region accessible to one observer. The cosmological budget~$\Ost$ of that region permits only a finite number of operations over its entire future~\cite{lloyd2000,lloyd2002,banks2001,krauss2004}. Infinite information is therefore accessible neither through measurement precision nor through the number of physical operations by which it can be obtained.

Ideas of finite precision and of a threshold have been discussed before. Buniy, Hsu, and Zee linked the finiteness of distinguishable states to the discreteness of Hilbert space and the quantization of its coefficients~\cite{buniy2005}, and then allowed wave-function components with norm below a discreteness threshold to be excluded in order to eliminate ``maverick branches'' and restore Born's rule~\cite{buniy2006}. This threshold was not fixed: it depended on the characteristic energy or the system size and the number of subsystems. In an algorithmic approach to quantum physics, Ozhigov cut off small amplitudes: given an available computational resource (algorithm steps or memory cells), amplitudes below the inverse square root of that resource, i.e.\ probabilities below its reciprocal, are set to zero; a fixed amplitude quantum leaves only finitely many nonzero components and is used to describe measurement and to recover Born's rule~\cite{ozhigov2004}. For a finite amplitude precision~$\mu$, Srikanth obtained a memory overflow ${\cal M}_{\max}=\mu\cdot 2^{\mu/2}$ bits as entanglement grows: unitarity becomes computationally unstable, and an information transition makes the system jump into a less complex factorized state according to the Born rule, with the basis taken from decoherence~\cite{srikanth2003,srikanth2006}. Davies compared the cosmological information bound $I_{\mathrm{universe}}\sim10^{122}$ bits with the number of complex coefficients of a generic $N$-qubit state and obtained the scale $N\approx\log_2 I_{\mathrm{universe}}\approx400$~\cite{davies2007}. Davies's number~$400$ follows from the hypothesis that the cost of a classical description of the state limits its physical existence. Del Santo and Gisin introduced a finite informational precision of physical quantities and discussed a collapse-like actualization of undefined digits, but in the context of classical indeterminism rather than quantum branch weights~\cite{delsanto2019}. In the Montevideo interpretation, Gambini, Garc\'ia-Pintos, and Pullin include the gravitational limit on the precision of clocks and rods: an event occurs when the residual interference can no longer be distinguished from a reduction (undecidability, not FAPP)~\cite{gambini2011}. Harlow and Hayden showed that quantum gedanken experiments with black holes may be computationally inaccessible within the available time~\cite{harlow2013}.

The new proposal of this work is the \CollapseRule{}: within a causal patch no more than~$\Ost$ binary distinctions are executable, so the weight of a branch cannot be smaller than $\varepsilon=1/\Ost$; a branch with a smaller weight vanishes, and a coherence below the same threshold vanishes in the same way (objectivation). The threshold is estimated from the number of operations executable in the causal patch over its entire future (\hyperref[sec:cosmological-limit]{\S~\ref*{sec:cosmological-limit}}, \hyperref[sec:computational]{\S~\ref*{sec:computational}}). We then define branches and their weight (\hyperref[sec:systems]{\S~\ref*{sec:systems}}), state the rule (\hyperref[sec:rules]{\S~\ref*{sec:rules}}) and its consequence for energy (\hyperref[sec:collapse-duration]{\S~\ref*{sec:collapse-duration}}). These are followed by the experimental test (\hyperref[sec:experiment]{\S~\ref*{sec:experiment}}), superluminal communication and the preferred frame (\hyperref[sec:signaling]{\S~\ref*{sec:signaling}}, \hyperref[sec:comoving]{\S~\ref*{sec:comoving}}), and predictions for quantum computers (\hyperref[sec:ft]{\S~\ref*{sec:ft}}). Existing experiments in which the action of the rule is imperceptible are treated in Appendix~\ref{sec:scales}.

\section{Cosmological Computational Limit}
\label{sec:cosmological-limit}

The calculation is carried out in the flat $\Lambda$CDM model: the FRW metric with $\Omega_k=0$, $\Omega_m+\Omega_\Lambda+\Omega_r=1$; the radiation contribution in the present epoch is negligible ($\Omega_r<0.01\%$).

We take the Hubble horizon $R=c/H$, the radius of the apparent horizon in flat FRW, and the second Friedmann equation for the energy density~$\rho$ and pressure~$p$
\begin{equation}
\dot H = -\frac{4\pi G}{c^2}(\rho+p).
\end{equation}
Horizon growth rate:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dR}{dt}
= -c\,\frac{\dot H}{H^2}
= \frac{4\pi G}{c^3}(\rho+p)\,R^2.
\end{equation}
Then $\rho=\rho_m+\rho_\Lambda$, $p_m=0$, $p_\Lambda=-\rho_\Lambda$, whence $\rho+p=\rho_m$. Therefore, in the adopted approximation, the entropy change and the available operations are determined by the rest energy of non-relativistic matter; the dark-energy contribution vanishes because $\rho_\Lambda+p_\Lambda=0$. For a flat universe $\rho_m=\Omega_m\cdot 3c^4/(8\pi G R^2)$, and
\begin{equation}
\frac{dR}{dt} = \frac{3}{2}\,c\,\Omega_m.
\end{equation}

The entropy of the apparent horizon is $S=\pi k R^2/\ell_P^2$, where $\ell_P=\sqrt{\hbar G/c^3}$ is the Planck length:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dS}{dt}
= \frac{2\pi k R}{\ell_P^2}\frac{dR}{dt}
= \frac{3\pi k c^4}{\hbar G}\,\Omega_m R.
\end{equation}
The matter energy inside the horizon is $E_m=c^4\Omega_m R/(2G)$. Lloyd's limit~\cite{lloyd2000,lloyd2002}
\begin{equation}
\dot N_m = \frac{2E_m}{\pi\hbar} = \frac{c^4\Omega_m R}{\pi\hbar G}.
\end{equation}
Dark energy produces no entropy ($\rho_\Lambda+p_\Lambda=0$). The ratio
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dot S}{k\,\dot N_m} = 3\pi^2
\end{equation}
--- $3\pi^2$~nats (about $42.7$~bits) per orthogonal matter operation. $\Omega_m$ and~$R$ cancel: the relation is holographic and epoch-independent while matter and~$\Lambda$ dominate.

In the future the horizon tends asymptotically to the de~Sitter horizon of radius~$R_{\mathrm{dS}}$, and its entropy $S_{\mathrm{dS}}=\pi k R_{\mathrm{dS}}^2/\ell_P^2$ is finite~\cite{gibbonshawking1977}. The identity $S_{\mathrm{dS}}-S=S_{\mathrm{dS}}\Omega_m$ is exact: $R_H^2=R_{\mathrm{dS}}^2\Omega_\Lambda$ for a flat universe without radiation. Remaining orthogonal operations
\begin{equation}
\Ost(t)
= \int_t^\infty \dot N_m\,dt'
= \frac{S_{\mathrm{dS}}-S}{3\pi^2 k}
= \frac{S_{\mathrm{dS}}\Omega_m}{3\pi^2 k}
\approx 3.5\times 10^{120}
\end{equation}
The value given here is for the present epoch at $\Omega_m=0.315$ and $H_0=67.4$~km/s/Mpc~\cite{planck2020}. The integral is finite because the horizon asymptotically tends to~$R_{\mathrm{dS}}$. The calculation uses the Hubble horizon; with the cosmological event horizon, which is now larger by~$15\%$, the remainder is about three times smaller and the limit on the number of qubits (\hyperref[sec:computational]{\S~\ref*{sec:computational}}) is lower by~$1.7$. This does not affect the conclusions, since this limit is an upper bound. In a homogeneous Friedmann--Robertson--Walker (FRW) cosmology the quantity~$\Ost(t)$ is the same for comoving observers at the same cosmological time.

As~$\Omega_m$ decreases, $\Ost(t)$ decreases: at recombination, neglecting radiation, $\Ost\approx 1.1\times 10^{121}$, now $\Ost\approx 3.5\times 10^{120}$, and in the asymptotic future $\Ost(t)\to 0$. On laboratory timescales the change in~$\Omega_m$ can be neglected.

This calculation refers to matter that disperses homogeneously. The limit on the number of qubits, $\log_2\Ost$ (\hyperref[sec:computational]{\S~\ref*{sec:computational}}), then decreases slowly: it will lose its next qubit in about~$4.6$~billion years, and in the matter era it was no higher than~$402$ qubits. The remainder tends to zero only in an empty region; there, not a single particle is left after about $1.2\times10^{12}$~years, and the question of superpositions no longer arises. Real matter clumps. A gravitationally bound mass~$M$ stays inside the horizon and keeps it from growing to the de~Sitter one: the entropy of the horizon stays below~$S_{\mathrm{dS}}$ by $2\pi k\,McR_{\mathrm{dS}}/\hbar$~\cite{bousso2002}. In this case it is natural to count the remainder from this entropy difference; it then tends not to zero but to $2McR_{\mathrm{dS}}/(3\pi\hbar)$. For the Local Group ($M\sim3\times10^{12}M_\odot$) this is about~$6\times10^{110}$, so this limit does not fall below about~$370$ qubits. The threshold is thus stable: over the next billions of years it changes by fractions of a qubit.

\section{Probability Threshold from the Computational Limit}
\label{sec:computational}

In quantum Darwinism, objectivity arises when information about the state of the system is reproduced many times in independent fragments of the environment~\cite{zurek2009}; this picture develops Wheeler's ``It from Bit''~\cite{wheeler1989}. A binary distinction is understood here in Wheeler's sense---a yes/no answer to a posed question, i.e.\ an act rather than a stored record.

The usual estimate of the information limit of a causal patch is its memory capacity, the holographic bound~\cite{bousso2002}: the area of the de~Sitter horizon in Planck cells, $N_{\mathrm{cell,max}}=4\pi R_{\mathrm{dS}}^2/\ell_P^2\approx1.3\times10^{123}$; the de~Sitter entropy in bits is smaller by a factor of~$4\ln2$, $4.8\times10^{122}$. This quantity shows how many binary facts fit in the region. $\Ost$ (\hyperref[sec:cosmological-limit]{\S~\ref*{sec:cosmological-limit}}) shows something else: how many binary distinctions are executable in it. The same gap between the horizon entropy and the processable budget was noted by Krauss and Starkman~\cite{krauss2004}: their limit on the energy that can be harvested in de~Sitter space gives the order~$10^{120}$, but not an identity for~$\Ost$. Hereafter the count~$\Ost$ is adopted: physically, a branch exists insofar as it can be distinguished from absence, not insofar as it can be stored.

The ratio of the two quantities is
\[
\frac{N_{\mathrm{cell,max}}}{\Ost}
=\frac{12\pi^2}{\Omega_m}
\approx 376.
\]
There is still enough memory, but not enough operations to distinguish all the addresses. On a logarithmic scale this factor lowers the limit from $\log_2 N_{\mathrm{cell,max}}\approx 409$ to $N_*=\log_2\Ost\approx 400.4$, i.e.\ by about $8.6$ qubits. The estimate of Davies~\cite{davies2007}, of the same order, is a count of memory capacity, not of executable distinctions, and it supplies no truncation rule.

The quantity~$\Ost$ is not a consumable quota distributed among devices, but the total number of binary answers the causal patch can give over its entire future. The quantum of weight is the share of one such answer:
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon = \frac{1}{\Ost}\approx 3\times 10^{-121}.
\end{equation}
An outcome with weight below~$\varepsilon$ gets less than one answer, that is, it never occurs. This is an estimate of scale, not a derivation of the exact value. $\Ost$ is computed from the Lloyd limit, that is, at the maximal rate of computation; real matter works more slowly, and one repetition of an experiment costs many operations. Therefore $1/\Ost$ is a lower bound on the quantum, and $N_*=\log_2\Ost\approx400$ is an upper bound on the wall. By the wall we mean the largest number of qubits~$N_w$ at which a register can still be in a superposition of all~$2^{N_w}$ equiprobable strings: with more qubits the weight of each string is below~$\varepsilon$. The dependence is logarithmic: each factor of~$1000$ in the number of available answers shifts the wall by~$10$ qubits. From below, the window is bounded by experiment: random circuit sampling (RCS) on~$67$ and~$83$ qubits shows no wall~\cite{morvan2024,gao2025}. We expect the wall to lie in the upper part of the window, closer to~$400$ qubits. All estimates below are tied to the upper bound $N_w=N_*$; if the wall lies lower, the estimates for circuits shift with it, since they depend only on the difference $N-N_w$. The quantum~$\varepsilon$ characterizes the resolving power of the entire region, has the same value for every entangled system, and does not grow with the number of devices. It is a property of the causal patch, not of the laboratory apparatus: the local environment determines which branches and coherences are tested, but not the value of~$\varepsilon$.

The threshold does not forbid improbable series. A particular series of~$500$ coin tosses has probability $2^{-500}<\varepsilon$, and such series occur in every experiment. This is a combinatorial probability: the product of the weights of five hundred separate events, each of weight~$1/2$ (\hyperref[sec:two-kinds]{\S~\ref*{sec:two-kinds}}). The same holds for a $500$-bit file written bit by bit. The threshold applies to a coherent probability: the weight of one term of a superposition, which exists as a whole.

\paragraph{What happens below the threshold.}
The threshold says that a weight smaller than~$\varepsilon$ is indistinguishable, but not what happens to a state that contains such a branch. There are three options. First: the branch vanishes and the weights of the others do not change. The probabilities then sum to less than unity, and in a fraction of the experiments nothing occurs, that is, the system disappears together with its charge and mass; this is not observed. Second: the appearance of even one such branch takes the whole system into a single outcome. But tails with weight below~$\varepsilon$ are present in almost any wave function, for example for the electron in a hydrogen atom beyond about $7.5$~nm from the nucleus (\hyperref[sec:tail]{Appendix~\ref*{sec:tail}}); there would then be neither stable atoms nor interference. Third: only the branches below the threshold vanish, and the probabilities of the others are counted among those that remain and sum to unity. This does not contradict experiment: for ordinary systems the weight of the vanishing tails is negligible, and the change is noticeable only where the small branches are many and their total weight is of order unity. This option is the one adopted (\hyperref[sec:rules]{\S~\ref*{sec:rules}}).

\section{Branches and Their Weight}
\label{sec:systems}

\subsection{Branch}
\label{sec:branch}

A branch of a quantum state is one of the terms that make it up and exist simultaneously. Which terms are counted is specified below: the way the state is written is set by the counting rule (\hyperref[sec:counting]{\S~\ref*{sec:counting}}), and within a part that does not decompose into factors the terms are the sectors of the einselection basis (\hyperref[sec:pointer]{\S~\ref*{sec:pointer}}). In Feynman's terms these are interfering alternatives~\cite{feynmanhibbs1965}.

For a single particle the branches are its alternatives in whatever the interaction distinguishes: energy level, spin projection, position. The branches in position are cells the size of the de~Broglie wavelength that corresponds to the characteristic momentum of the state. The state contains nothing finer: a measurement that localizes the particle more precisely imparts momentum to it and is a different operation with its own branches (\hyperref[sec:weight]{\S~\ref*{sec:weight}}).

\subsection{Two Kinds of Probability}
\label{sec:two-kinds}

The ordinary probability familiar from classical physics is combinatorial: the probability of a compound event is the product of the probabilities of separate events or separate systems. Such is the probability of a series of coin tosses, of a chain of successive measurements, and of a microstate of a heated body: the entropy of a body counts the number of ways of composing it from separate systems. In quantum mechanics such is the probability of a joint string of a product $|\Psi\rangle=\bigotimes_a|\psi_a\rangle$: each factor has its own state and its own branches, and the probability of a string is the product of their weights. We call such a probability combinatorial.

Quantum mechanics also has a probability of another kind, a coherent one: the probability of one of the alternatives that exist simultaneously and interfere, that is, the probability of a branch. We call it the weight of a branch and use this term below; ``coherent probability'' and ``weight of a branch'' mean the same thing. Branches belong to a separate system and to a state that does not decompose into factors: in such a state the reduced state of a part is mixed~\cite{nielsenchuang2010}, the part has no branches of its own, its alternatives enter the branches of the whole, and a term exists only as a whole. No branch corresponds to a combinatorial probability: a string of a product is not a branch but a set of branches of different systems, one from each, and its probability is the product of their weights.

A probability is combinatorial not because it can be written as a product: any number can be written so. For example, the weight~$2^{-500}$ of one string of a Haar state equals~$1/2$ multiplied by itself five hundred times. What matters is what the factors correspond to: a probability is combinatorial only if each factor is the weight of a branch of a separate system or of a separate completed event. Thermal probabilities would be coherent only if all the particles of the body were in one pure state that does not decompose into factors.

Put simply, branches exist simultaneously: both one and the other. In a product this ``and'' refers to each qubit separately: the first is both~$0$ and~$1$, the second is both~$0$ and~$1$, and a string is composed of their alternatives, one from each. In a state that does not decompose into factors, the ``and'' stands between whole strings.

\subsection{Counting Rule}
\label{sec:counting}

The state is written as a sum of products with the smallest number of terms. A factor that is not itself a product is written in the same way, and its terms are counted separately from the terms of the other factors. Each term of this expansion is a branch with its own weight; the weight of a nested term is taken together with the weight of the term that contains it. The product $|+\rangle^{\otimes N}$ is one term, and each qubit has two branches of its own of weight~$1/2$; the probability~$2^{-N}$ of a string is combinatorial. A GHZ state has two terms of weight~$1/2$. The state in Grover's algorithm~\cite{grover1996} also has two: the sought string and the uniform superposition. A Haar state~\cite{page1993} has of the order of~$2^N$ terms of weight of the order of~$2^{-N}$; the calculations for it below use the strings of the pointer basis. The three cases are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:systems}.

\subsection{Einselection Basis}
\label{sec:pointer}

Within a term or a factor that does not decompose further into products, a branch is a sector of the einselection basis with projector~$P_i$ and Born weight $p_i=\Tr(P_i\rho)$; the off-diagonal blocks $P_i\rho P_j$ describe interference between branches. The sectors are set by physical interaction with the environment, and their number depends on how the environment is coupled to the system. If it acts on each qubit separately, the sectors are strings, and there are~$2^N$ of them. If it distinguishes only a total quantity, for example the total number of atoms in the upper level, there are $N+1$ sectors, and a branch is a value of this quantity, not a list of atoms. For a system weakly coupled to its environment the pointer states are the energy eigenstates~\cite{pazzurek1999}; the computational strings of an idle register are exactly such states.

\subsection{Effective Number of Branches}
\label{sec:keff}

The number of branches of a state that does not decompose into factors, with their weights taken into account, is measured by the inverse participation ratio (IPR)~\cite{thouless1974,grobe1994}:
\begin{equation}
\Keff \;=\; \frac{1}{\sum_i p_i^2}.
\end{equation}
For~$K$ equal weights $p_i=1/K$ we get $\Keff=K$. For a Haar state in a space of dimension~$D=2^N$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_i p_i^2\right]=\frac{2}{D+1},
\qquad
\Keff\approx\frac{D+1}{2}\approx2^{N-1}
\end{equation}
(using the concentration of the IPR at large~$D$). For the three states of Fig.~\ref{fig:systems}: in the product each factor has $\Keff=2$, the GHZ state has $\Keff=2$, and the Haar state has $\Keff\approx2^{N-1}$.

\subsection{When the Weight of a Branch Is Defined}
\label{sec:weight}

The Born weight of a branch is the frequency of an outcome in an experiment that can detect this branch. An experiment is an operation with a completed result. Until the operation that creates a branch is completed, there is no such experiment: a measurement made in the middle of the operation is a different, shorter operation with its own branches and its own weights. The weight of a branch is therefore defined once the operation that created it is completed. An operation is completed when the system has become experimentally distinguishable from what it was. This refers to the whole system, not to a single qubit: a register of~$N$ qubits becomes distinguishable from its previous state when each qubit has gone through about~$1/\sqrt N$ of the gate, so about~$\sqrt N$ operations are completed in one layer of gates. The count does not depend on how many pulses a gate is divided into. In one operation a parent passes to new branches a fraction of its weight of order unity.

\subsection{Entanglement without Branching}
\label{sec:phase}

Entanglement does not always mean branching. Take two qubits in a product: four strings, $00$, $01$, $10$, and~$11$, each with probability $1/4=1/2\cdot1/2$. A controlled phase shift changes the sign in front of the string~$11$. The qubits are now entangled, but the weights of the strings are the same, and each still equals the product of the weights of the two qubits. Such an operation changes only the phases of the terms and creates no new branches: the probabilities remain combinatorial. The same holds for any state obtained from a product by such operations, however many qubits it contains: for a chain of qubits entangled with their neighbors by a controlled phase shift, for a cloud of atoms in which collisions shift the phases (\hyperref[sec:fountain]{Appendix~\ref*{sec:fountain}}), and, after a suitable rotation of the basis of each qubit, for the states of error-correcting codes~\cite{vandennest2004}.

\section{\CollapseRule}
\label{sec:rules}

\subsection{Postulate}
\label{sec:postulate}

A description of a physical state contains no more than~$\Ost$ independently distinguishable elements (\hyperref[sec:computational]{\S~\ref*{sec:computational}}), and the weight of each branch is an integer number of quanta $\varepsilon=1/\Ost$. A weight smaller than one quantum cannot be represented: there is no such branch in the description. This is not an action on the state but an absence: the branch is not removed, it vanishes. By description we mean what experiment can distinguish: the Born weights of branches and the interference between them. The rule acts on the full density matrix of all entangled particles, in the einselection basis, without a system/environment split. The weights lie on the grid $n_i/\Ost$ with integer~$n_i$ (for the upper bound; at $N_w<N_*$ the grid step is $2^{-N_w}$); between zero and~$\varepsilon$ there is no admissible value, so the excess is shed by the vanishing of branches rather than by coarse-graining all the weights. The closest analogue is fixed-point arithmetic, where a number smaller than the grid step gives machine zero; the difference is that here probability is counted among the existing branches, so the share of each of them grows. The calculations below use only the threshold; the discreteness of weights above the threshold does not affect the results. The full dynamical model is discussed in \hyperref[sec:discussion]{\S~\ref*{sec:discussion}}.

The rule combines three processes. Truncation: branches below the threshold vanish, and the others remain in superposition. Objectivation: when the residual coherence falls below the threshold, a single outcome remains. Selection: if all branches are below the threshold, a single random one remains. Objectivation and selection are collapse in the usual sense; truncation is not.

\subsection{What the Threshold Applies To}
\label{sec:applies}

The quantum~$\varepsilon$ applies to coherent probabilities, that is, to the weights of branches (\hyperref[sec:two-kinds]{\S~\ref*{sec:two-kinds}}): it is compared with the weight of each term in the expansion with the smallest number of terms. A combinatorial probability can be arbitrarily small, because the threshold applies to each of its factors separately. In Fig.~\ref{fig:systems} the probability of a string of the product is not bounded by the threshold, the weight of a term of the GHZ state is above the threshold, and the weight of a term of the Haar state at $N\gtrsim400$ is below it. The threshold does not apply to thermal probabilities or to states obtained from a product by phase shifts. The vanishing of a nested term reduces the weight of the term that contains it. If nothing is left of a term, it vanishes as a whole when the sum has other terms; if it is the only term, the degenerate case applies. If the weights of all branches are not below~$\varepsilon$, the model predicts nothing new.

\subsection{Vanishing of Branches}
\label{sec:vanishing}

A branch (\hyperref[sec:branch]{\S~\ref*{sec:branch}}) is absent from the description if its weight is smaller than the quantum, $p_i<\varepsilon$: over the whole budget of~$\Ost$ answers it occurs on average $\Ost p_i$ times, that is, never. A record in the environment does not make a branch an outcome: the system is entangled with the environment, and the branch belongs to the larger system that includes the environment.

The threshold refers to the weight, not to the amplitude, and this is part of the postulate, not a consequence of counting operations. Amplitude amplification~\cite{grover1996,brassard2002} raises the probability to order~$\nu^2p_i$ in~$\nu$ steps, so in standard quantum mechanics the budget~$\Ost$ would allow weights down to~$\varepsilon^2$ to be distinguished. In the model, amplification does not rescue a small branch: the branch vanishes before it can be amplified (\hyperref[sec:when]{\S~\ref*{sec:when}}). A grid on amplitudes would give a threshold of~$\varepsilon^2$ and a wall around $2N_*\approx800$ qubits; experiment distinguishes these two variants.

The quantum of weight refers to each finest sector, not to a sum of sectors. Therefore the vanishing of many such branches changes outcome probabilities noticeably: their total weight can be of order unity. Experiment detects the very fact that the branches are absent, although the weight of each of them is unmeasurable.

It follows that entanglement has a smallest portion. In standard quantum mechanics systems that have ever interacted, even through intermediaries, remain slightly entangled. Here a term with weight below~$\varepsilon$ vanishes, so two systems are either entangled by a term with weight not below~$\varepsilon$ or their state is an exact product; the smallest entanglement is about $\varepsilon\log_2(1/\varepsilon)\approx10^{-118}$~bit. Two experiments linked only by negligible entanglement are therefore counted separately: the terms with weight below~$\varepsilon$ vanish, and in each of the remaining ones the state of the two experiments is a product.

\subsection{Irreversibility of the Vanishing of Branches}
\label{sec:irreversible}

Since $\Ost(t)$ decreases monotonically, $\dot\varepsilon>0$: the threshold only rises with time. A branch that vanished at an earlier value of the threshold remains below it and, by \CollapseRuleD{}, is not restored. Unitary evolution may subsequently create a branch with the same quantum numbers, but it will be a new branch, not a restoration of the vanished one.

\subsection{Renormalization}
\label{sec:renorm}

Let $\Sinv=\{i:\ p_i<\varepsilon\}$ be the set of invisible sectors and $\Pi=I-\sum_{i\in \Sinv}P_i$ the projector onto the existing part of the state. If its weight is positive, what remains after the invisible branches vanish is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{N}(\rho)
=\frac{\Pi\rho \Pi}{\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho \Pi)}.
\end{equation}
This is the truncation~$\mathcal N$. Only an existing branch can occur in a measurement, so the probability of an outcome equals the share of its weight among the existing branches, $p'_i=p_i/\sum_{j\notin \Sinv}p_j$. The existing branches themselves do not change: their relative amplitudes and phases stay the same. Hermiticity and positivity are preserved: what remains is a principal submatrix of a positive semidefinite matrix~\cite{hornjohnson2013}. A cutoff of individual matrix elements by~$|\rho_{ij}|<\varepsilon$ is not applied: it may violate positivity.

The weight of each existing branch is no smaller than the quantum, so after truncation the effective number of branches (\hyperref[sec:keff]{\S~\ref*{sec:keff}}) is bounded: $\Keff\le\Ost$, that is, $\log_2\Keff\le N_*\approx400.4$; for~$K$ equiprobable alternatives this is $K\le\Ost$. The condition applies to each state that does not decompose into factors separately: for a product $\rho=\bigotimes_a\rho_a$, to each factor, $\Keff(\rho_a)\le\Ost$, and not to the product as a whole or to sequential classical recording.

\subsection{Dephasing}
\label{sec:dephasing}

After the invisible branches vanish, several existing sectors generally remain; this is not yet a single outcome. The residual coherence of the full matrix in the einselection basis is measured by the trace distance to the mixture~$\Delta_P(\rho)$:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_P(\rho)=\sum_i P_i\rho P_i,
\qquad
C(\rho)=\frac12\bigl\|\rho-\Delta_P(\rho)\bigr\|_1.
\end{equation}
For two orthogonal branches with weights $p$ and $1-p$ one has $C=\sqrt{p(1-p)}$. Unitary entanglement with the environment moves inter-branch blocks into correlations and does not by itself reduce $C$ of the full~$\rho$ or raise its von Neumann entropy. For the coherence of the existing sectors to fall to~$\varepsilon$, dephasing of the full matrix is required; the mechanism may vary. As an example we take the hypothesis of independent proper-time clocks (BDPT)~\cite{ponfilenok2026bdpt}: the blocks $P_a\rho P_b$ decay as $e^{-\chi}$, $\chi=(E_a^2+E_b^2)\,\delta t^2/2\hbar^2$, where $E_a$ and~$E_b$ are the energies of those degrees of freedom in which the branches differ, and $\delta t$ is the uncertainty of proper time. For masses separated in space, this is the rest energy~$mc^2$, and $\chi\propto(mc^2)^2t^{2/3}$; for the internal levels of a qubit, it is the transition energy. Radiation and $\Lambda$ energy do not enter the phase. As the blocks decay, the entropy of the full~$\rho$ grows toward~$S\bigl(\Delta_P(\rho)\bigr)$. The scales are as follows: for~$400$ qubits with a transition frequency of~$5$~GHz over~$1$~$\mu$s, with two hundred excited qubits in each branch, $\chi\sim10^{-36}$, and coherence is unaffected; for a record of mass~$1$~pg separated over two branches, $C$ falls below~$\varepsilon$ in a time of order~$3\times10^{-18}$~s. This is a separate hypothesis, not a consequence of~$\Ost$, and no numerical result of this work depends on it: the dephasing mechanism affects only the moment at which a single outcome appears.

\subsection{Objectivation}
\label{sec:objectivation}

When $C(\rho)\le\varepsilon$, the difference between~$\rho$ and the mixture~$\Delta_P(\rho)$ is indistinguishable within the~$\Ost$ budget: the off-diagonal blocks are zeroed, and the description becomes~$\Delta_P(\rho)$. While $C>\varepsilon$, interference is retained in the description. This is a proper mixture of the full density matrix, not an improper mixture obtained by tracing out the environment, in which a subsystem looks mixed while the global superposition still exists. A single outcome already exists but is unknown; this ignorance is the von Neumann entropy of the mixture, $S\bigl(\Delta_P(\rho)\bigr)\ge S(\rho)$, so objectivation is a growth of physical entropy. Almost all of it is accumulated by dephasing: at $C\le\varepsilon$ the zeroing itself adds at most of order~$\varepsilon\log K$, by the continuity of entropy~\cite{fannes1973,audenaert2007}, where $K\lesssim\Ost$ is the number of existing sectors. The mapping~$\Delta_P$ does not shift the weights~$\{p_i\}$, and a Loschmidt echo~\cite{swingle2018} does not restore the zeroed off-diagonal terms. For a branch it is its weight that is compared with the quantum, and for coherence it is the trace distance to the mixture; the choice of these two measures is part of the postulate.

\subsection{Degenerate Case}
\label{sec:degenerate}

If \emph{all} branches are invisible, their joint vanishing would zero~$\rho$, and a proper mixture of invisible branches is inadmissible. Then selection acts,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal C^{\mathrm{sel}}(\rho)
=\frac{P_k\rho P_k}{p_k},
\qquad
\Pr(k\mid \Sinv)=\frac{p_k}{\sum_{i\in \Sinv}p_i}.
\end{equation}
This is not objectivation: the trigger is the absence of a visible remainder, not~$C\le\varepsilon$. It arises for a flat distribution: any state of~$2^m$ branches with equal weights~$2^{-m}$ at $m>N_w$, for the upper bound at $m\ge401$, is invisible as a whole, and a single random string remains. This is the general branching wall; it determines the fate of Shor's algorithm (\hyperref[sec:shor]{\S~\ref*{sec:shor}}). If a system has at least one branch with a weight not below~$\varepsilon$, the case is not degenerate: the invisible branches vanish and that branch remains, however small its weight.

\subsection{When Truncation Acts}
\label{sec:when}

The condition $p_i\ge\varepsilon$ refers to the weights defined once an operation is completed (\hyperref[sec:weight]{\S~\ref*{sec:weight}}); the rule requires no separate act of comparison. This removes the Zeno paradox~\cite{misra1977}: during an operation the weight of a new branch grows from zero, but until the operation is completed it is not a weight in the sense of the rule, and the branch does not vanish at birth. An operation cannot be shorter than the uncertainty of proper time, $\delta t\approx(t_P^2\,t)^{1/3}$, where $t$ is the superposition interval and $t_P=\sqrt{\hbar G/c^5}\approx5.4\times10^{-44}$~s is the Planck time~\cite{karolyhazy1966,ponfilenok2026bdpt}. Truncation acts after each completed operation. The next operation mixes the remaining branches anew, and some of them again fall below the threshold. The more operations there are, therefore, the more weight is lost, and branches begin to vanish already at a larger weight: each doubling of the number of operations raises this weight by a factor of about~$\sqrt2$, that is, shifts the wall by half a qubit. In a register of~$400$ qubits about twenty operations, not one, are completed in a layer of gates; this is about four doublings, and the wall sets in about two qubits earlier. The estimate comes from a calculation for small registers. The exact formulation for arbitrary continuous evolution remains open (\hyperref[sec:discussion]{\S~\ref*{sec:discussion}}).

\subsection{Front of the Wall for a Random Circuit}
\label{sec:pruning}

Random circuit sampling (RCS)~\cite{boixo2018,arute2019} prepares a state close to Haar: all~$N$ qubits enter one state that does not decompose into factors, and the branches are the joint strings~$|x\rangle$ of the register in the einselection basis, with weights $p_x=|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^2$ (\hyperref[sec:pointer]{\S~\ref*{sec:pointer}}). The rule compares with~$\varepsilon$ the weight of each string. Truncation acts after each completed operation (\hyperref[sec:when]{\S~\ref*{sec:when}}), before readout, and readout fixes the already altered distribution. Objectivation does not occur here: for a coherent superposition of many strings $C$ is of order unity, and BDPT dephasing for qubits over microseconds is negligible ($\chi\sim10^{-36}$, \hyperref[sec:dephasing]{\S~\ref*{sec:dephasing}}).

The weights of the strings obey the Porter--Thomas distribution~\cite{porter1956,boixo2018}, $P(p)=2^N e^{-2^N p}$. The circuit depth~$d$ is the number of sequential layers of two-qubit gates; the distribution is established at the saturation depth $d_{\mathrm{sat}}\sim\sqrt{N}$, at which the light cone covers the entire chip, and in shallower circuits the vanishing of branches starts later. The total weight of the vanishing branches, $W=\sum_{i:\,p_i<\varepsilon}p_i$, is obtained by integrating the distribution up to the threshold~$p_{\mathrm{thr}}$:
\begin{equation}
W(u)
\;=\;
1-(1+u)e^{-u}
\;\simeq\;
\frac{u^2}{2},
\qquad
u=2^N p_{\mathrm{thr}}.
\end{equation}
Here the threshold is $p_{\mathrm{thr}}=\varepsilon$ and $u=2^{N-N_*}$. At a sensitivity $\delta=10^{-3}$ the effect becomes visible around $N=396$ qubits. At $N\approx400.4$ a weight $W=1-2/e\approx26.4\%$ vanishes; at $N=403$ it is already $W\approx98\%$.

The vanishing weight~$W$ must be distinguished from the number of remaining strings. The fraction of strings with $p_i\ge\varepsilon$ equals $e^{-u}$, whence
\begin{equation}
n_{\mathrm{vis}} = D e^{-u} = 2^N \exp\bigl(-2^{N-N_*}\bigr).
\end{equation}
The limit $W\to1$ means only that almost all of the weight has gone into the tails below~$\varepsilon$, not that one string remains: at $N=405$ one still has $n_{\mathrm{vis}}\sim10^{111}$. A single string remains at $n_{\mathrm{vis}}=1$, that is, at
\begin{equation}
\Ncl\ln2 = 2^{\Ncl-N_*},
\qquad
\Ncl\approx408.5
\end{equation}
The transition therefore extends from $N\approx400$ to $\Ncl\approx408.5$ (for the upper bound on the wall): at its beginning an appreciable fraction of the weight vanishes, and at its end the pre-measurement state is a single string.

\section{Non-conservation of Energy at Truncation and Selection}
\label{sec:collapse-duration}

Truncation and selection are not unitary, so they do not guarantee conservation of the mean energy. The moment of the jump is defined no more sharply than the uncertainty of proper time (\hyperref[sec:when]{\S~\ref*{sec:when}}); on laboratory scales it is instantaneous.

\paragraph{Truncation energy.}
The mean energy of the state is $\langle H\rangle=\Tr(H\rho)$. After the invisible sectors $\Sinv=\{i:p_i<\varepsilon\}$ vanish, we have $\mathcal{N}(\rho)=\Pi\rho \Pi/\Tr(\Pi\rho \Pi)$, and the energy shift is defined as
\begin{equation}
\Delta E=\Tr(H\mathcal{N}(\rho))-\Tr(H\rho).
\end{equation}
This is not a new assumption but a consequence of the renormalization rule (\hyperref[sec:renorm]{\S~\ref*{sec:renorm}}). Let the sectors be approximately energy eigensectors, $[H,P_i]\approx0$ (this holds for the strings of an idle register, \hyperref[sec:pointer]{\S~\ref*{sec:pointer}}), and let $E_i=\Tr(HP_i\rho P_i)/p_i$ be the mean energy of branch~$i$. Then $\Tr(H\rho)=\sum_i p_i E_i\equiv\langle E\rangle$, $\Tr(H\mathcal{N}(\rho))=\langle E\rangle_{\bar{\Sinv}}$ and $\Tr(H\Pi\rho\Pi)=\sum_{i\notin \Sinv}p_i E_i$, where $\bar{\Sinv}$ is the complement of~$\Sinv$. Denoting $W=\sum_{i\in \Sinv}p_i$, $\langle E\rangle_{\Sinv}=(\sum_{i\in \Sinv}p_i E_i)/W$ and $\langle E\rangle_{\bar{\Sinv}}=(\sum_{i\notin \Sinv}p_i E_i)/(1-W)$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:branch-energy}
\Delta E=W\bigl(\langle E\rangle_{\bar{\Sinv}}-\langle E\rangle_{\Sinv}\bigr)=\langle E\rangle_{\bar{\Sinv}}-\langle E\rangle.
\end{equation}
The shift is finite for any $W<1$ and does not exceed the spread of energies over the branches: $|\Delta E|\le W|\langle E\rangle_{\bar{\Sinv}}-\langle E\rangle_{\Sinv}|$. Under partial truncation it is nonzero only if the vanished and the existing branches differ systematically in~$E_i$. Truncation only removes branches, so at $[H,P_i]\approx0$ no energy values appear that were absent from the state: only the mean shifts. $H$ includes everything that exchanges energy with the system. In the local marginal test (\hyperref[sec:marginal]{\S~\ref*{sec:marginal}}) the scrambled arm carries on average about two hundred ancilla excitations, but the drive has given up the same amount in that arm; the total energies of the arms are equal, and the vanishing of an arm does not change this. In the degenerate case the selection~$\mathcal C^{\mathrm{sel}}$ gives $E_k-\langle E\rangle$ in a single act and zero on average over the Born distribution: there is no systematic sink or source, unlike in GRW/CSL models~\cite{ghirardi1986,ghirardi1990,bassi2013}, where the parameter~$\lambda$ is limited by spontaneous heating. Whether the random selection of an outcome produces heat in the environment, as the erasure of information does according to Landauer~\cite{landauer1961}, is not considered here.

\section{Experimental Test}
\label{sec:experiment}

The main testable prediction of the model is the wall (\hyperref[sec:computational]{\S~\ref*{sec:computational}}): at about four hundred qubits the branches of a random circuit vanish (\hyperref[sec:pruning]{\S~\ref*{sec:pruning}}). We propose four different experiments to test this on one processor of $410$--$412$ qubits: the mirror circuit (\hyperref[sec:mirror]{\S~\ref*{sec:mirror}}), paired circuits (\hyperref[sec:pair]{\S~\ref*{sec:pair}}), the local marginal test (\hyperref[sec:marginal]{\S~\ref*{sec:marginal}}), and a search on a small register (\hyperref[sec:small-search]{\S~\ref*{sec:small-search}}). The third is the main one: a single qubit is measured in it, and superluminal communication follows from it (\hyperref[sec:signaling]{\S~\ref*{sec:signaling}}).

The absolute value $\varepsilon\sim 10^{-121}$ is not accessible to direct measurement in the laboratory. We therefore propose to capture the geometry of the boundary, the break between two chains, and the nonlinear signal of the local marginal test---a shift of one qubit's probabilities without measuring the entire register. The scan runs over the number of qubits~$N$, the circuit depth~$d$, and the chain structure. These are not three independent threshold parameters: the \CollapseRule{} compares branch weights with~$\varepsilon$; $N$, $d$, and the structure only determine which distribution~$\{p_i\}$ the circuit prepares. In RCS after saturation~$d\gtrsim d_{\mathrm{sat}}$ the wall lies no higher than~$N\approx400$; a shallower circuit shifts it in~$d$, and narrow branching at the same~$N$ and~$d$ gives no wall. The model is refuted if a wide entangled state in the pointer basis behaves according to the standard theory at~$N$ well above~$N_*$. As long as processors grow and no wall appears, the window for~$N_w$ narrows from below.

\paragraph{Boundary geometry.}
The boundary here is a line on the plane of the number of qubits~$N$ and the depth~$d$ beyond which the signal of the circuit is lost. Ordinary noise gives one such boundary: under independent errors the signal is lost when the number of gates exceeds the error budget, that is, roughly on the hyperbola $N\cdot d\approx\mathrm{const}$. This is an approximation of the error-accumulation model; the working control curve is built from the measured noise channels. The \CollapseRule{} gives a second boundary, of a different shape: at $d<d_{\mathrm{sat}}$ it depends on the depth, and after the Porter--Thomas distribution is established at $d\gtrsim d_{\mathrm{sat}}$ it runs almost vertically, at the number of qubits equal to the position of the wall; for the upper bound, in the region $N\approx400\pm3$. The front~$W(u)$ (\hyperref[sec:pruning]{\S~\ref*{sec:pruning}}) is shown by the orange curve in Fig.~\ref{fig:front}; $u$ doubles with each qubit. At $u\ll1$ we have $W\simeq u^2/2$, so $W$ grows roughly fourfold per qubit; in the transition window ($N\sim400$) the growth of $W$ slows. The formula refers to a single truncation. After saturation each further layer mixes the remaining strings anew, and truncation is repeated (\hyperref[sec:when]{\S~\ref*{sec:when}}). The front therefore depends on depth, but weakly: with each doubling of the number of operations after saturation it shifts by about half a qubit toward a smaller number of qubits. The position of the wall~$N_w$ is determined by the scan; the dependence on depth follows from the same formula applied repeatedly and is not tuned. The position of this vertical boundary should not shift when the ordinary error rate is reduced, unlike the noise boundary.

\paragraph{Residual noise.}
Does residual noise push some of the qubits into the mode of sequential classical recording? An error event records information about the state of roughly one qubit and may entangle it with matter in the environment, in which case the rest energy of that matter enters the dephasing of the branch; the mean number of such events is $\langle k\rangle = N d e_{2q}/2$. At the working point $N\approx400$, $d=40$, $e_{2q}=10^{-4}$, this is $\langle k\rangle\approx0.8$---less than one qubit out of four hundred, with the shift of the wall within the window $N\approx400\pm3$.

\subsection{Mirror Circuit}
\label{sec:mirror}

The idea is a random-circuit echo: a register prepared in $|0\rangle^{\otimes N}$ is run through the scrambler~$U$ and the reverse mirror~$U^\dagger$; under standard unitary evolution the state returns to zero, while under the \CollapseRule{} branches vanish inside~$U$ and the compensation breaks down. The metric is the return probability $F=\bigl|\langle 0|U^\dagger U_{\mathrm{phys}}|0\rangle\bigr|^2$~\cite{swingle2018}. Predicting~$F$ classically is not required: it suffices to run $U U^\dagger$ repeatedly on the chip and measure the fraction of returns to $|0\rangle^{\otimes N}$. Poisson statistics require $M\sim 100/F$ copies. Under independent stochastic two-qubit gate noise a rough estimate is $F\approx e^{-N d e_{2q}}$ (of order $N d/2$ gates in each of the two passes). At $e_{2q}=3\times 10^{-3}$, $d=40$, $N\sim400$ this is $F\sim10^{-21}$, which practically closes the test; at $e_{2q}=10^{-4}$ it is of order $0.20$. The testability condition is $e_{2q}\lesssim 5/(N d)$ (so that $F\gtrsim10^{-2}$). Randomized compiling~\cite{wallman2016} separates stochastic noise from coherent compensation. The sensitivity~$\delta$ of the mirror circuit sets the lower edge of the window: at $\delta=10^{-3}$ the vanishing weight becomes visible around $N=396$ qubits.

\paragraph{Discrimination from hardware noise.}
At fixed $N$, $d$, and circuit structure, the ordinary noise boundary should shift when the measured~$e_{2q}$ changes, whereas the position of the threshold front in~$N$ is set by~$\varepsilon$ and does not shift; lowering~$e_{2q}$ only changes the ability to see this front. Therefore repeating the scan on devices or calibrations with different~$e_{2q}$ is the main distinguishing test. Unknown correlated noise that does not follow the measured~$e_{2q}$ remains a competing explanation~\cite{kalai2016,rinott2022}.

\subsection{Paired Circuits}
\label{sec:pair}

The comparison is made on one chip at the same~$N$ and the same depth~$d$; the circuits differ in chain structure, not in depth. The branching width is set by the number of terms of the state (\hyperref[sec:counting]{\S~\ref*{sec:counting}}) \emph{at each layer}, not by the number of entangled qubits. The wide circuit consists of random single-qubit rotations and two-dimensional layers of two-qubit gates (RCS). The superposition fills almost all~$2^N$ strings, and for the Haar distribution $\Keff\approx2^{N-1}$ after~$d\gtrsim d_{\mathrm{sat}}$. In the narrow circuit $\Keff$ is small \emph{throughout} the circuit. A classical reversible network (CNOT, SWAP on computational states) holds one string, $\Keff=1$. A CNOT chain after one~$H$ gives GHZ: all qubits are entangled, but there are two existing branches, $\Keff=2$. The echo~$U^\dagger U$ is \emph{not} a narrow path: in the middle of the first~$U$ the distribution is already Haar, and the threshold fires before the unwinding. In the corresponding transition region the narrow circuit works normally; the difference between the two circuits reveals the geometry of the wall. The signature is a residual deviation of the wide circuit that does not disappear when ordinary errors are reduced.

\subsection{Local Marginal Test}
\label{sec:marginal}

The idea of the experiment is as follows. Qubit~$\qA$ is entangled with the control qubit~$\qC$ in a Bell pair, and the state of $N$~ancillas depends on~$\qC$: at~$\qC=0$ they remain in zeros, at~$\qC=1$ they are scrambled over~$2^N$ strings. The state is a sum of two terms, or arms: a narrow one, with a single string of the ancillas and weight~$1/2$, and a wide one, in which the weight of each string is below~$\varepsilon$. The wide arm vanishes as a whole, the narrow one remains, and qubit~$\qA$ is always found in zero, whereas by standard quantum mechanics it is in one in half of the cases. The arms must differ in width: if the ancillas are scrambled in advance, independently of~$\qC$, the strings vanish equally in both arms and there is no shift. Therefore~$\qC$ first creates the difference between the arms, and only then are the ancillas scrambled.

The reference circuit with a conditional scrambler has $404$ qubits on one chip---the control~$\qC$, $402$ ancillas, and a qubit~$\qA$ entangled with~$\qC$ in a Bell pair. Qubit~$\qC$ is not measured. The bit is encoded by the operation: $Z$-control is a controlled-$U$; $X$-control is $H$ on~$\qC$, a controlled-$U$, and $H$ back. The conditional circuit on the ancillas is the identity at~$\qC=0$ and the scrambler~$U$ at~$\qC=1$; the mirror~$U^\dagger$ is not needed---the marginal is fixed at the moment of truncation. The metric is the marginal of qubit~$\qA$: $\rho_{\qA}=\Tr_{\qC,\mathrm{anc}}(\rho)$, i.e.\ $P(\qA{=}0)$ and $P(\qA{=}1)$.

In standard quantum mechanics both operations are local with respect to~$\qA$, and its marginal is strictly~$1/2$. In the model, under $Z$-control a fraction $W(2u)$ of the scrambled arm vanishes, and after renormalization
\begin{equation}
P_Z(\qA{=}1)=\frac{1-W(2u)}{2-W(2u)},
\qquad
P_X(\qA{=}1)=\tfrac12,
\end{equation}
where $u=2^{N-N_*}$. Under $X$-control the scrambled branches are symmetrically linked to $\qA=0$ and~$\qA=1$; the vanishing of branches shifts both outcomes equally. \paragraph{Implementation without conditional gates.}
\label{sec:unconditional}
A conditional scrambler requires every gate to depend on~$\qC$; implemented directly, this means auxiliary qubits to fan out the control and three-qubit gates. It is simpler to make the initial state conditional; this is the circuit proposed for the experiment. Let the scrambler~$U$ be built from gates that conserve the number of excitations: two-qubit gates of the iSWAP type and single-qubit phase rotations. The rotation angles must be arbitrary: with rotations by a quarter turn only, the circuit consists of Clifford gates, its states reduce to ones obtained from a product by phase shifts, and they contain no branching (\hyperref[sec:phase]{\S~\ref*{sec:phase}}). Then $U|0\ldots0\rangle=|0\ldots0\rangle$. First, qubit~$\qC$ flips half of the ancillas by a chain of CNOTs, as in the preparation of a GHZ state; then the same~$U$ acts unconditionally in both arms (Fig.~\ref{fig:circuit}). In the arm $\qC=0$ the ancillas stay all zero; in the arm $\qC=1$ the state spreads over the sector with~$N/2$ excitations, whose dimension is $\binom{N}{N/2}\approx2^{N-4.7}$. At $N=408$ this is $2^{403.3}$ strings, slightly more than for $403$ ancillas in the reference circuit, with $P_Z(\qA{=}1)\approx4\times10^{-6}$; $410$ qubits are needed in total. Such a scrambler mixes more slowly than a Haar one, because excitations spread diffusively~\cite{khemani2018,rakovszky2018}; the saturation depth may exceed~$\sqrt N$, and a margin of a few ancillas makes the circuit insensitive to deviations from the Porter--Thomas distribution.

\paragraph{Control: block scrambler.}
The same circuit provides a control that distinguishes a coherent probability from a combinatorial one. Divide the ancillas into blocks and remove the gates between blocks from~$U$. With~$440$ ancillas and ten blocks of~$44$, the number of joint strings in the arm $\qC=1$ is~$2^{409}$, and the weight of each is below~$\varepsilon$. But at~$\qC=1$ the state of the ancillas is a product of the states of the blocks, and the probability of a joint string is combinatorial (\hyperref[sec:two-kinds]{\S~\ref*{sec:two-kinds}}): for each block the all-zero string has weight~$1/2$, the others about~$2^{-42}$, and nothing vanishes. The model predicts $P_Z(\qA{=}1)=1/2$ for the block scrambler and $P_Z(\qA{=}1)\to0$ for the full one on the same qubits. The number of strings is above the wall in both cases; the only difference is whether the state of the arm decomposes into factors.

\subsection{Search on a Small Register}
\label{sec:small-search}

The marginal test is the case of two arms of equal weight. The same mechanism works for unequal weights, and this gives a fourth experiment. A register of~$\ell$ qubits is put into a superposition of all~$2^\ell$ candidates, and a simple reversible circuit marks one of them with a flag. The flag plays the role of the control qubit: on the candidates without the flag it flips half of the ancillas with a chain of CNOT gates, after which the scrambler acts unconditionally (\hyperref[sec:unconditional]{\S~\ref*{sec:unconditional}}). The state is a sum of two terms: the marked candidate with the ancillas in zeros, whose weight is~$2^{-\ell}$, and the other candidates with scrambled ancillas. The second term vanishes as a whole, and after renormalization the marked candidate remains (Fig.~\ref{fig:search}). At $\ell=3$ it occurs in one case out of eight by standard quantum mechanics, and almost always by \CollapseRuleD{}. The processor is almost the same as in the marginal test: three qubits of the register and the flag are added to~$408$ ancillas, $412$~qubits in all. With this number of ancillas the marked candidate is found in about~$99\%$ of the cases instead of~$12.5\%$; this estimate is for a single truncation. The same experiment on a register of hundreds of qubits is a key search; it requires a fault-tolerant computer and is treated in \hyperref[sec:search]{\S~\ref*{sec:search}}.

\section{Superluminal Telephone}
\label{sec:signaling}

Once the local marginal test (\hyperref[sec:marginal]{\S~\ref*{sec:marginal}}) is confirmed, the same circuit is moved to a separated baseline of length~$l$: qubit~$\qA$, entangled with~$\qC$ by a Bell pair, is delivered to Bob in advance over telecom fiber, and the effect becomes a communication channel. The working point for the upper bound of the wall is $404$ qubits ($402$ ancillas; $\qC$ and~$\qA$ are not included in~$N$). Alice does not measure~$\qC$; the bit is encoded by $Z$- or $X$-control over the entire ensemble of~$M$ acts. Bob measures~$\qA$ in the~$Z$ basis.

In ordinary quantum mechanics, Alice's local unitary operations on~$\qC$ and the ancillas do not change $\rho_{\qA}$ at Bob: when measuring~$\qA$ in the~$Z$ basis he always sees $P(\qA{=}1)=50\%$, regardless of Alice's choice between $Z$- and $X$-control. In the model, some global branches of the system $\qC+\text{ancilla}+\qA$ vanish, and probabilities are counted among the existing ones (truncation~$\mathcal N$, \hyperref[sec:renorm]{\S~\ref*{sec:renorm}}). That is the superluminal shift of the marginal. $Z$- and $X$-control yield \emph{different} full matrices~$\rho$, not two ensembles of the same~$\rho$. On Alice's isolated chip, $C$ does not fall to~$\varepsilon$ within microseconds: only the transition energy of the qubits enters the BDPT dephasing (\hyperref[sec:dephasing]{\S~\ref*{sec:dephasing}}), and there are no copy-records. Rare error events, fewer than one per run on average (\hyperref[sec:experiment]{\S~\ref*{sec:experiment}}), put about one qubit out of four hundred into the recorded mode and do not cause objectivation of the whole register. Selection at Bob fixes the already shifted bit. At $N=402$:
\begin{equation}
P_Z(\qA{=}1)=\frac{1-W(2u)}{2-W(2u)}\approx1.62\%,
\qquad
P_X(\qA{=}1)=\tfrac12,
\end{equation}
a difference of $\approx48.4$ percentage points.

The signal arises precisely from the \emph{absolute} threshold. If the threshold depended only on relative weights, renormalization would not change Bob's observable probabilities. In GRW-type collapse models the averaged evolution is linear, so different preparations of one~$\rho$ are indistinguishable and the superluminal channel is closed~\cite{ghirardi1986,ghirardi1990,bassi2013}. For Weinberg's nonlinear quantum mechanics, Gisin and Polchinski showed that local operations on one side of an EPR pair change the statistics on the other~\cite{weinberg1989,gisin1990,polchinski1991}.

Bob's frequency of ones is the mean of $M$ coin tosses with probability~$q$; its width equals $\sqrt{q(1-q)/M}$. For the $\pm 2\sigma$ intervals around~$P_Z(\qA{=}1)$ and~$1/2$ not to overlap,
\begin{equation}
\bigl|P_Z(\qA{=}1)-\tfrac12\bigr|
\ge
2\Bigl(\sqrt{P_Z(\qA{=}1)\bigl(1-P_Z(\qA{=}1)\bigr)/M}+\sqrt{1/(4M)}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
Hence at $N=402$ we obtain $M\approx7$ acts per bit. On a two-dimensional lattice, saturation takes $d_{\mathrm{sat}}\sim\sqrt{N}\approx20$ layers (\hyperref[sec:pruning]{\S~\ref*{sec:pruning}}), which at a layer duration of tens of nanoseconds, as in processors on superconducting qubits, gives $t_{\mathrm{run}}\sim0.5$~$\mu$s per act; one bit then takes a few microseconds, and the transmission rate is of the order of hundreds of kbit/s. All numbers in this section are order-of-magnitude estimates for superconducting qubits; on ions and neutral atoms a layer of gates lasts several orders of magnitude longer, and the rate is correspondingly lower. They refer to the scrambler itself and do not include the conditional preparation (of the order of~$\sqrt N$ layers) or a possible increase in depth (\hyperref[sec:unconditional]{\S~\ref*{sec:unconditional}}); this is an upper bound on the rate. Each act consumes one Bell pair~$\qC$--$\qA$ (after the readout of~$\qA$ new entanglement is needed), so a stable channel requires a pair distribution rate $R_{\mathrm{Bell}}=1/t_{\mathrm{run}}\sim10^6$ pairs/s; at a lower~$R_{\mathrm{Bell}}$ the real transmission rate is limited by~$R_{\mathrm{Bell}}/M$, not only by~$t_{\mathrm{run}}$ on the chip. A rate of~$10^6$ pairs/s is orders of magnitude more than present quantum communication lines provide, so the rate will be limited by the delivery of pairs, not by the chip. The readout of qubit~$\qA$ at Bob takes about~$100$~ns and is not included in~$t_{\mathrm{run}}$.

The superluminal signal itself is a truncation jump (\hyperref[sec:collapse-duration]{\S~\ref*{sec:collapse-duration}}), simultaneous on the FRW layer (\hyperref[sec:comoving]{\S~\ref*{sec:comoving}}), not the continuous $\chi(t)$. The channel beats light if $M\cdot t_{\mathrm{run}}<l/c$; the minimum baseline is of the order of a kilometer. The local marginal test remains on one $404$-qubit chip (a baseline of centimeters); the race with light is set by separating~$\qA$ and Alice's station by at least that distance after the effect is confirmed. Synchronization of control and readout on a unified comoving time scale is described in~\hyperref[sec:comoving]{\S~\ref*{sec:comoving}}.

\paragraph{Two-way communication.}
Two-way communication requires symmetric $404$-qubit setups at each station. In an Alice$\to$Bob tick, $\qC$ and the ancillas are at Alice and $\qA$ is at Bob; in a Bob$\to$Alice tick the roles swap. With one pair one cannot transmit and receive simultaneously, so opposing ticks are separated on the comoving layer (\hyperref[sec:comoving]{\S~\ref*{sec:comoving}}).

\section{CMB Frame}
\label{sec:comoving}

The model \emph{assumes} that the quantity~$\Ost$ and the simultaneity of the truncation and selection jumps are set not by laboratory clocks but on a layer of constant cosmological time of the homogeneous isotropic Friedmann--Robertson--Walker (FRW) metric---the standard geometry of the expanding Universe. In this idealization the layer $t_{\mathrm{FRW}}=\mathrm{const}$ is one and the same for the entire metric: all comoving observers (zero peculiar velocity) agree on simultaneity. In practice, the same preferred frame is the rest frame of the cosmic microwave background (CMB); it is to this frame that the model attaches the jump, not to the local simultaneity of each laboratory and not to the continuous $\chi(t)$ of the BDPT hypothesis (\hyperref[sec:dephasing]{\S~\ref*{sec:dephasing}}). A setup on Earth moves relative to the CMB at a speed $v\approx370$~km/s, so its Einstein or GPS synchronization is shifted from this layer by $vl/c^2$ (see below). Entangled pairs are separated subluminally and remain inside one causal patch, so the operation budget is defined over the entire experiment. In inhomogeneous or rotating geometries a global comoving layer is not naturally defined. Compatibility with the constraints on Lorentz-invariance violation~\cite{mattingly2005,liberati2013} is not discussed here. The open questions of extending the CMB layer to local systems, of the preferred frame, and of causal consistency are discussed in \hyperref[sec:discussion-comoving]{\S~\ref*{sec:discussion-comoving}}.

\subsection{Preferred-Layer Test}
\label{sec:comoving-test}

The shift $vl/c^2$ exceeds one readout time~$\tau$ at $l\gtrsim(c^2/v)\tau$; for $\tau\sim100$~ns this is $l\gtrsim24$~km. The events are launched \emph{deliberately simultaneously} in the laboratory. Then the orientation of the baseline relative to the CMB dipole decides whose recording crosses the FRW layer first, and Bob's statistics should oscillate with the \emph{sidereal} period ($\approx23$~h~$56$~min), not the solar day. The solar-day drift (temperature, grid) is filtered out by this period.

The Alice$\to$Bob channel is not switched off: if Alice launches the measurement earlier than Bob in the laboratory by a margin greater than~$vl/c^2$ (at $24$~km this is~$\sim100$~ns), she is also earlier on the FRW layer at any time of day.

This is a separate protocol, not a race with light: it verifies that the preferred layer coincides with rest relative to the CMB. Laboratory simultaneity is set by Einstein synchronization (in practice GPS/GNSS or two-way fiber transmission); it differs from the CMB layer precisely by the shift $vl/c^2$.

\subsection{Synchronization by Comoving Clocks}

The model postulates the simultaneity of collapse on the CMB layer, so to schedule the control and readout moments, local clock readings are converted to the cosmological time~$t_{\mathrm{FRW}}$. For nearby stations on Earth the correction to first order equals $\Delta t_{\mathrm{CMB}}\simeq\mathbf{v}\!\cdot\!\Delta\mathbf{x}/c^2$: for a baseline of~$\sim1$~km this is about~$4$~ns, small compared with an act of~$\sim0.5$~$\mu$s. At space distances one additionally integrates the gravitational potential and the kinematic time dilation along each worldline. This is a conversion of clock readings to the assumed preferred frame, not a separate physical signal; its accuracy is limited by the ephemerides, the model of the gravitational field, and the extension of the CMB layer to inhomogeneous geometries (\hyperref[sec:discussion-comoving]{\S~\ref*{sec:discussion-comoving}}).
\section{Predictions for Quantum Computers}
\label{sec:ft}

The consequences in this section are also testable, but they require a fault-tolerant computer with hundreds of logical qubits, not a processor of~$410$ qubits (\hyperref[sec:experiment]{\S~\ref*{sec:experiment}}).

\subsection{Shor's Algorithm}
\label{sec:shor}

The influence of the threshold is determined not by the number of qubits as such but by the number of terms of the state (\hyperref[sec:counting]{\S~\ref*{sec:counting}}). Consider the stages of the standard Shor algorithm~\cite{shor1997}. Let~$m$ be the width of the counting register, $Q=2^m$, and $n$ the modulus to be factored. After the Hadamard transforms the counting register remains a product of single-qubit states (\hyperref[sec:two-kinds]{\S~\ref*{sec:two-kinds}}), so $2^{-m}$ is a combinatorial probability, not the weight of one branch; branches do not vanish. Modular exponentiation entangles the registers:
\begin{equation}
|\psi\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt Q}\sum_{k=0}^{Q-1}|k\rangle\,|a^k\bmod n\rangle.
\end{equation}
The number of terms of this state is not less than the Schmidt rank between the counting and work registers: the Schmidt rank is the smallest number of products of the form ``a state of the counting register times a state of the work register'' in which the state can be written, and the expansion with the smallest number of terms (\hyperref[sec:counting]{\S~\ref*{sec:counting}}) cannot be shorter. The quantity $a^k\bmod n$ depends only on $k\bmod r$, where $r$ is the order of the base~$a$, so the rank equals $\min(Q,r)$, the number of distinct values in the work register, and the weights in the Schmidt decomposition are close to~$1/\min(Q,r)$. If $\min(Q,r)>2^{N_w}$, the weight of every term is below~$\varepsilon$ however the state is written.

If the order~$r$ is small or is a power of two, the terms are few and the wall does not fire. For an RSA modulus $n=pq$ of~$L$ bits the order of a typical base is comparable to~$n$, that is, $\log_2 r\approx L$, and the fraction of bases whose order is a power of two is negligibly small, so these cases do not weaken the conclusion about cryptographic moduli. These cases provide a null control for a test on a fault-tolerant processor: at the same~$m$ and~$d$ a run with~$r=2^j$ or with small~$r$ should succeed, while a run with large $r\ne2^j$ should fail no later than $m\approx400$.

The \CollapseRule{} is imposed during the circuit, not only on the final state. Modular exponentiation is implemented by sequential controlled-$U$ operations on one control at a time; after~$t$ steps the entangled block contains $\min(2^t,r)$ values of the work register with equal weights, while the unprocessed controls remain factors in~$|+\rangle$. When $\min(2^t,r)$ exceeds~$2^{N_w}$, all terms of the block are below~$\varepsilon$, the degenerate case applies (\hyperref[sec:degenerate]{\S~\ref*{sec:degenerate}}), and the block collapses to one string $|k_0\rangle|a^{k_0}\rangle$; the algorithm breaks down already in the middle of the exponentiation. A single string carries no periodic structure, and the subsequent QFT gives an almost uniform spectrum instead of peaks at multiples of~$Q/r$.

The failure criterion is therefore
\begin{equation}
\min(m,\log_2 r)>N_w,\qquad N_w\le N_*\approx400.4.
\end{equation}
The standard Shor algorithm for an $L$-bit modulus uses $m=2L$, and $\log_2 r\approx L$, so it certainly fails at $L\gtrsim400$. This bound does not depend on the layout of the counting register: in the variant with one recycled control qubit~\cite{beauregard2003} there is no counting register, but the work register holds the same superposition of~$r$ values with weights~$1/r$. The Schmidt rank gives only a lower estimate of the number of terms: if the terms were all the strings of both registers, the failure would set in already at $L\approx201$ ($m=402$); where between $201$ and~$400$ bits the boundary for the standard algorithm lies remains undetermined. For RSA-512 the standard algorithm \emph{fails} in the model.

However,~$m$ need not equal~$2L$. The Ekerå--Håstad schemes~\cite{ekera2017} reduce RSA factoring to a short discrete logarithm and use a counting register of width $m\approx L/2+L/s$ with a tradeoff parameter~$s$; the number of values in the work register is then~$2^m$. For RSA-512 at $s\ge4$ this gives $m\le384<N_*$: if the wall lies at the upper bound, the weights of the terms remain above the threshold and the modulus is factored; if $N_w\le384$, this scheme fails as well. The fate of RSA-512 therefore depends on the position of the wall; only standard period finding is certainly forbidden. Since $m\ge L/2$ for any~$s$, all known period-finding schemes for RSA-1024 and RSA-2048 fall in the zone $m>N_*$; the period-finding boundary lies around $L\gtrsim800$.

In a fault-tolerant scheme there may be far more physical qubits than~$N_*$, but the limit concerns not their number but the logical structure of the algorithm---the width~$m$ of the logical counting register. Syndrome measurements record only errors in the environment and do not reveal the logical state (stabilizers commute with logical operators), so the period-finding strings are not by themselves converted to the classical recorded mode. For the same reason the estimate $\langle k\rangle$ does not apply to the logical register: correction does not let the environment accumulate a record of the logical state specifically.

\subsection{Other Algorithms}
\label{sec:other-alg}

Phase estimation (QPE) with a coherent counting register wider than~$N_*$ falls under the general branching wall (\hyperref[sec:degenerate]{\S~\ref*{sec:degenerate}}) if the target state is spread over~$d$ eigenvectors with $\log_2 d$ of the order of~$m$; Shor's algorithm is the special case $d=r$. The prohibition does not cover independent systems (\hyperref[sec:two-kinds]{\S~\ref*{sec:two-kinds}})---$|+\rangle^{\otimes N}$, the unprocessed controls in Shor's algorithm---and narrow Ekerå--Håstad registers. Nor does it cover states of a few terms (\hyperref[sec:counting]{\S~\ref*{sec:counting}}): QPE on an eigenvector, where the counting register remains a product; amplitude estimation~\cite{brassard2002}, where there are two eigenvectors and two terms; Grover search~\cite{grover1996}, whose state at every step is a sum of two products, $|+\rangle^{\otimes N}$ and the sought string.

\subsection{Search with Verification}
\label{sec:search}

The failure of Shor's algorithm (\hyperref[sec:shor]{\S~\ref*{sec:shor}}) means that this method of solution stops working, not that the problem itself becomes unsolvable. The same rule that breaks Shor's algorithm gives another way to find the answer: search with verification. This is the experiment with a small register (\hyperref[sec:small-search]{\S~\ref*{sec:small-search}}), carried over to a large one: a register of~$\ell$ qubits is in a superposition of all candidates, a reversible circuit sets a flag on the correct one, on the candidates without the flag a scrambler mixes~$N_a$ ancillas, and the term with the wrong candidates vanishes as a whole (\hyperref[sec:applies]{\S~\ref*{sec:applies}}). The verification itself does not touch the wall: it involves $2^\ell$ strings of weight~$2^{-\ell}$. There are two conditions. The correct candidate must be visible: $2^{-\ell}\ge\varepsilon$, that is, $\ell\le N_w$. The term with the wrong candidates must vanish as a whole: the fraction of its weight that remains above the threshold, $(1+u)e^{-u}$ with $u=2^{N_a-N_w}$, must be much smaller than~$2^{-\ell}$. For~$\ell$ of the order of~$N_w$ this requires about~$9$ ancillas more than the width of the wall: $N_a\ge409$ for the upper bound, where this fraction is~$2^{-551}$. This estimate is for a single truncation; with repeated truncations (\hyperref[sec:marginal]{\S~\ref*{sec:marginal}}) fewer ancillas suffice. Unlike the experiment with a small register, the verification circuit here is large, for example AES encryption, and requires a fault-tolerant computer.

The consequences are as follows. A key of up to~$N_w$ bits for which a check is known is found in a single run; this applies, for example, to AES-256 given known pairs of plaintext and ciphertext. An RSA-1024 modulus is factored by a search over the $256$ high bits of a factor: by Coppersmith's theorem~\cite{coppersmith1997}, knowledge of the high $L/4$ bits of a factor allows the modulus to be factored classically in polynomial time. This path works for $L/4\le N_w$, that is, no further than $L\simeq1600$. For RSA-2048 it needs~$512$ bits and fails: the correct candidate is itself below the threshold. This does not make RSA-2048 secure: other reductions of factoring to search are not examined here. It is known that nonlinearity and postselection give computational power that the standard theory lacks~\cite{abramslloyd1998,aaronson2005}.

\section{Discussion and Open Questions}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Existing Experiments}
\label{sec:existing}

Experiments in which hundreds, thousands and millions of particles are entangled do not contradict the model: the number of particles is not the number of branches. Five such experiments are treated in Appendix~\ref{sec:scales}: the tail of the electron wave function in a hydrogen atom, a cesium fountain, a chain of~$2000$ superconducting qubits, a squeezed ensemble of atoms in a cavity, and multiple-quantum NMR. In all of them the rule acts, but its action is imperceptible. Ground states of magnets and superconductors are energy eigenstates, that is, one branch (\hyperref[sec:pointer]{\S~\ref*{sec:pointer}}). A direct conflict with the model would arise upon demonstration of a state close to a Haar state on more than~$N_w$ qubits; the experiments known to us contain no such state.

\subsection{Comparison with GRW/CSL}

The GRW/CSL models~\cite{ghirardi1986,ghirardi1990,bassi2013} have a plane of free parameters $(\lambda,r_c)$, and mass enters the spontaneous collapse rate; here the lower bound of the weight quantum, $\varepsilon=1/\Ost$, is not a fitted parameter, and mass enters only through the BDPT dephasing (\hyperref[sec:dephasing]{\S~\ref*{sec:dephasing}}). The structural choices---the einselection basis, renormalization (\hyperref[sec:renorm]{\S~\ref*{sec:renorm}}), and the width measure---remain. Two further differences: there is no systematic heating at the selection of an outcome (\hyperref[sec:collapse-duration]{\S~\ref*{sec:collapse-duration}}), and the averaged evolution is nonlinear, which is what makes superluminal communication possible (\hyperref[sec:signaling]{\S~\ref*{sec:signaling}}).

\subsection{Integer Micromodel}
\label{sec:micromodel}

The postulate and the grid of weights $n_i/\Ost$ are given in \hyperref[sec:postulate]{\S~\ref*{sec:postulate}}. A full microtheory has not yet been derived from this sketch. The weights of a product of subsystems, of the form $n_i m_j/\Ost^2$, do not lie on the grid, but they are combinatorial probabilities and need not lie on it (\hyperref[sec:two-kinds]{\S~\ref*{sec:two-kinds}}). Open obstacles: as $\Ost(t)$ decreases (\hyperref[sec:cosmological-limit]{\S~\ref*{sec:cosmological-limit}}) the entire discrete scale of distinguishability is rebuilt; the moment at which the weight of a branch is defined is fixed for operations with a definite completion (\hyperref[sec:weight]{\S~\ref*{sec:weight}}) but is not defined for continuous evolution without distinguished operations. Until these are resolved, the \CollapseRule{} specifies an effective one-step rule on the full density matrix of an entangled system, implementing the postulate for einselection objects, not a closed discrete quantum mechanics with an unambiguous evolution~$\rho(t)$.

\subsection{The Jump at the Threshold}

The rule is discontinuous: a branch with a weight just below~$\varepsilon$ vanishes, and one just above it remains. This almost never produces observable jumps. If one branch crosses the threshold, the probabilities of the others change by an amount of the order of~$\varepsilon$, which cannot be noticed. If many branches cross it, their weights differ, they do not cross it simultaneously, and the total vanished weight changes smoothly, by the formula for the front (\hyperref[sec:pruning]{\S~\ref*{sec:pruning}}). A genuine jump exists in one place, at the boundary of the degenerate case (\hyperref[sec:degenerate]{\S~\ref*{sec:degenerate}}). Let all branches have almost the same weight, close to~$\varepsilon$. If all of them are just below the threshold, a single random string remains; if one of them is just above it, that one remains, with no randomness. An arbitrarily small change of the weights changes the outcome. This discontinuity follows from the quantization of weight. It remains open whether it will persist in a full dynamical model (\hyperref[sec:micromodel]{\S~\ref*{sec:micromodel}}) or will prove to be a property of the one-step rule.

\subsection{Local Bekenstein Bound}

In this work the threshold is determined by the global causal-patch budget and is the same for all entangled systems. An alternative microtheory is possible in which the available precision is additionally limited by the Bekenstein bound $S\le 2\pi ER/(\hbar c)$ for a region containing the physical carriers of the entanglement~\cite{bousso2002}. For a compact system this local memory limit may be stricter than the cosmological operation budget; then $\varepsilon$ and the critical qubit number $N_*$ will depend on the size and energy of the setup. Whether a quantum state should be treated as locally stored information or as information of the entire causal patch remains an open question.

\subsection{Palmer Invariant Set}

A related idea is Palmer's invariant set~\cite{palmer2009}: reality is limited to a fractal invariant set. Here the limitation is different: the operational distinguishability of states within a causal patch, not the geometry of an attractor.

\subsection{Branches}

An absolute threshold requires unambiguously defined branches. The difficulties of defining and counting them are discussed by Saunders~\cite{saunders2021}. There is as yet no generally accepted precise definition of a branch~\cite{riedel2025}; definitions through redundant records~\cite{riedel2017} and through complexity~\cite{weingarten2022,taylor2025} have been proposed. The definition adopted here is given in \hyperref[sec:branch]{\S~\ref*{sec:branch}}. Probabilities are set by the absolute Born weights $p_i=\Tr(P_i\rho)$ in the einselection basis, not by the number of branches; the rule is a function of a single~$\rho$. The contrast between $Z$- and $X$-control in the local marginal test and in its separated realization arises not from two decompositions of one~$\rho$ into ensembles but from different unitary operations that change~$\rho$ itself; the dependence on the coarse-graining level remains an open question. In practice, the weight threshold acts on the pointer strings of the full matrix, while $C$ and BDPT act on already truncated sectors; whether these partitions always coincide has not been shown. For many weakly coupled systems the expansion with the smallest number of terms is not written out here: the counting rule and both limits are given (\hyperref[sec:counting]{\S~\ref*{sec:counting}}). The geometric measure of entanglement~\cite{wei2003} can serve as a measure of the distance from a product. In this paper the terms are set by the state itself, through the counting rule (\hyperref[sec:counting]{\S~\ref*{sec:counting}}), and the branches within a part that does not decompose into factors are set by the environment (\hyperref[sec:pointer]{\S~\ref*{sec:pointer}}). Whether these two ways agree in all cases has not been shown: for Haar states they give the same result, while in general they may differ.

\subsection{CMB Frame and Causality}
\label{sec:discussion-comoving}

In \hyperref[sec:comoving]{\S~\ref*{sec:comoving}} the simultaneity of the truncation and selection jumps is postulated on a layer of constant FRW time; on the cosmological background it coincides with the CMB rest frame. For an inhomogeneous gravitational field, no unambiguous extension of this layer from the cosmological background to local Earth and spacecraft systems is defined. The required precision of the conversion of the stations' proper times to~$t_{\mathrm{FRW}}$ has not been estimated. The preferred layer sets a single order of remote events and may thereby exclude causal loops arising under Lorentz-invariant superluminal transmission only if all nonlinear dynamics strictly obey this foliation; the causal consistency of the full microtheory has not yet been shown.

\section{Experiments in Which the Action of the Rule Is Imperceptible}
\label{sec:scales}

The rule always acts, but in most experiments its action is imperceptible. For each experiment below we state the weights of which branches are compared with~$\varepsilon$, what the rule predicts, and when its action would become noticeable.

\subsection{Spatial Tail of the $1s$ State}
\label{sec:tail}

The probability density of the $1s$ state is $e^{-2r/a_0}/(\pi a_0^3)$, where $a_0$ is the Bohr radius. The characteristic momentum of the electron is~$\hbar/a_0$, the de~Broglie wavelength is $\lambda_{\mathrm{dB}}=2\pi a_0\approx0.33$~nm, and the weight of a cell of this size at distance~$r\gg a_0$ from the nucleus is
\begin{equation}
w(r)\approx\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{dB}}^3}{\pi a_0^3}\,e^{-2r/a_0}=8\pi^2e^{-2r/a_0}.
\end{equation}
The condition $w<\varepsilon$ holds at $r\gtrsim7.5$~nm. Beyond that there are no branches of this size: in a measurement of position with a resolution of one cell the electron will not be found there, whereas in standard quantum mechanics the probability is small but not zero. For the coarsest measurement, which asks only whether the electron is beyond a given distance, the weight of the outcome is the weight of the whole tail, $e^{-2r/a_0}(1+2r/a_0+2r^2/a_0^2)$, and it becomes smaller than~$\varepsilon$ at $r\approx7.6$~nm: beyond this distance the electron will not be found in any measurement of position. The result depends only weakly on the cell size: even for the finest possible cell, the Compton wavelength $h/m_ec\approx2.4$~pm, the boundary moves only to~$7.1$~nm. The total weight of the vanished cells is of the order of~$10^{-118}$, so the truncation does not affect the rest of the state, and experiment will not detect it. The tail vanishes both before the position is recorded by the environment and after it, as long as objectivation has not occurred (\hyperref[sec:objectivation]{\S~\ref*{sec:objectivation}}). If the interaction distinguishes not the position but the energy level, the ground state is one branch; for an isolated atom the two descriptions give the same observables to within this weight.

\subsection{Weakly Entangled Ensemble}
\label{sec:fountain}

In a cesium fountain, a cloud of millions of atoms in a superposition of hyperfine levels flies between the two Ramsey pulses for about half a second~\cite{wynands2005}. Collisions shift the phase of an atom depending on the states of its neighbors~\cite{gibble1993}, so the state of the cloud is not an exact product. The shift is small compared with the width of a Ramsey fringe, about~$1$~Hz: the phase~$\phi_{\mathrm{col}}$ accumulated per atom during the flight is much less than a radian, and the interference contrast of a single atom differs from unity by an amount of the order of $\phi_{\mathrm{col}}^2\ll1$. What interferes is the two levels of each atom, each of weight about~$1/2$ (\hyperref[sec:two-kinds]{\S~\ref*{sec:two-kinds}}); this is what is observed as the Ramsey fringes on which the clock operates. The probability~$2^{-N}$ of a joint string of the whole cloud, with millions of atoms, is much smaller than~$\varepsilon$, but, as in a product, it is combinatorial and is not compared with the threshold. The rule compares with~$\varepsilon$ the weight of each branch of each atom, about~$1/2$: none vanishes, and the prediction coincides with the standard one, Ramsey fringes of the same contrast. The action of the rule would become noticeable if the collisions not only shifted the phases but also mixed the states of more than four hundred atoms into one state that does not decompose into factors; this does not happen in a fountain.

\subsection{Chain with Short-Range Correlations}
\label{sec:chain}

In the experiment of King et al., a chain of~$2000$ superconducting qubits is driven through a quantum phase transition in $5$--$50$~ns~\cite{king2022}. In the final state, neighboring qubits point the same way except at rare kinks, pairs of neighbors that point in opposite directions. At the fastest anneal the correlation length is $\xi\approx15$ qubits, and there is on average one kink per~$\xi$ pairs of neighbors: a probability of about~$0.066$. A typical complete result contains about~$130$ kinks, and its probability in the approximation of independent kinks is of order~$2^{-700}$, that is, below~$\varepsilon$. But stretches farther apart than a few~$\xi$ are not linked to each other, and neighboring ones are linked weakly: the kink correlations are reproduced by a calculation for a chain of~$256$ qubits in which no more than~$5$ bits of entanglement pass through a cut. What interferes is the alternatives of a stretch of length of the order of~$\xi$, with weights of order~$2^{-5}$, while the probability of a result of the whole chain is combinatorial (\hyperref[sec:two-kinds]{\S~\ref*{sec:two-kinds}}). The rule compares with~$\varepsilon$ the weights of the alternatives of a stretch: none vanishes, and the prediction coincides with the standard one. Experiment agrees: the kink density at~$2000$ qubits is the same as at~$512$ and matches the calculation for a closed system. If the strings of the whole chain were interfering alternatives of one state, only results with at most~$53$ kinks would remain, in the same approximation. The action of the rule would become noticeable when of the order of four hundred bits of entanglement pass through a cut of the chain. The closest to this are the annealing experiments on two- and three-dimensional lattices~\cite{king2023,king2025}, where the link between parts grows with the area of the cut. In the 2025 work the lattices contain up to~$567$ qubits, and the state is reproduced to the accuracy of the processor itself, by the authors' estimate, at a bond dimension of $10^{7}$--$10^{11}$, that is, at $23$--$37$ bits through a cut; the terms of the Schmidt decomposition at the cut carry a weight far above~$\varepsilon$. The weights of the strings were not measured in these experiments, as in the multiple-quantum NMR experiments (\hyperref[sec:nmr]{Appendix~\ref*{sec:nmr}}), and it is to them that the threshold applies.

\subsection{Collectively Coupled Ensemble}
\label{sec:squeezed}

In the experiment of Hosten et al., half a million rubidium atoms are coupled equally to the mode of a common cavity, and the spread in the number of atoms in the upper level is squeezed by~$20$~dB, that is, its variance is~$100$ times smaller than for independent atoms~\cite{hosten2016,hosten2016a}. The cavity distinguishes only this number, so the sectors are its values (\hyperref[sec:pointer]{\S~\ref*{sec:pointer}}), and the weight of each is of order~$10^{-2}$. The rule compares with~$\varepsilon$ the weights of these sectors: none vanishes, and the squeezed state persists, as observed. Counting by lists of atoms would give weights far below~$\varepsilon$, and the squeezed state would have to vanish. The action of the rule would become noticeable if the environment distinguished each atom separately.

\subsection{Multiple-Quantum NMR}
\label{sec:nmr}

In multiple-quantum NMR experiments in solids~\cite{krojanski2004,krojanski2006,alvarez2015}, in adamantane the evolution of the spins is reversed by pulses, and the number of correlated spins is determined from the returning signal; $650$, about~$4900$ and about~$7000$ spins have been reported. This quantity shows how far the correlation has spread, and the authors call it effective. The distribution of the weights of the strings is not measured in such experiments, and it is to this distribution that the threshold applies: what matters is not the number of entangled particles but the weights of the branches, which depend on how close the state is to a Haar state. A GHZ state and a Haar state of the same number of spins give coherences of the same highest order, although the first has two terms and the second of the order of~$2^N$. The fraction of the signal that returns at the largest sizes is not given in these works. The model predicts that it must fall sharply when the number of strings with appreciable weight exceeds~$2^{N_w}$; a measurement of this fraction would test the model on existing setups. How the number of strings with appreciable weight is related to the measured number of correlated spins is not established here.
